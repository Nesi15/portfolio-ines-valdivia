100 años de la Revolución

100 Years of the Revolution. A documentary about the Guna community of Panama, filmed on the centenary of the 1925 Dule Revolution and told in their own voices: identity, memory and resistance.

26 min · 2025 Final degree project Pre-production, production and post-production: Inés Valdivia

How it started

I arrived in Guna Yala during my stay in Panama thanks to the Universitat Politècnica de València's development cooperation grant. A conversation with members of the community about the Dule Revolution and the importance of cultural preservation became the starting point of my final degree project.

What it is

100 años de la Revolución is an intimate, observational documentary filmed on the islands of Guna Yala, marking the centenary of the revolution that secured the autonomy of the Guna people. It does not set out to speak about the community, but with it: their own voices tell of cultural identity, collective memory and the challenges they face today.

Discover its history

A journey through the events that led to the 1925 Dule Revolution and Guna self-government, told with images from anthropologist James Howe's archive and period photographs.

  1. 1699

    The first European accounts

    English surgeon Lionel Wafer lives among the peoples of the isthmus and publishes one of the region's first ethnographies. On European maps, the archipelago appears as the “Islas de las Mulatas”.

  2. 1870

    The “Tule Indians”

    The document Civilización de los indios tules, published in Bogotá, describes 36 villages between San Blas Bay and the Gulf of Urabá, their civil and religious authorities, and the role of the nele, the ritual specialists.

  3. 1903–1913

    Panama sets out to “civilise”

    After splitting from Colombia in 1903, the new republic wants control of the territory. President Belisario Porras sends military and diplomatic expeditions up the Mandinga River, which the Guna see as an invasion of their land.

  4. 1913

    The missions arrive

    English missionary Anna Coope opens a school on Narganá. The missions teach in English and Spanish and push women and children to cut their hair, give up the mola and abandon the Dulegaya language.

  5. 1914

    Porras in Ustupu

    The president visits the congress house in Ustupu, the Guna political and ceremonial centre, and meets Nele Kantule. Behind the courtesy, he insists on “integrating” the Guna and banning molas, beadwork and face paint.

  6. 1915

    The “taking possession” of San Blas

    Panama raises its flag over the archipelago, appoints a civil authority and sets up police posts on the islands. The new rules sharply raise tensions.

  7. 1919–1924

    Repression

    Official reports treat resistance as rebellion and accuse Simral Colman of defying the state. The colonial police impose public punishments, arbitrary arrests and abuse of women.

  8. 1923–1924

    Outside witnesses

    American William Markham travels the islands and in 1924 writes to Porras denouncing the abuses. Richard O. Marsh lives in Ustupu and Ailigandí, befriends Nele Kantule and Simral Colman and reports to his government.

  9. February 1925

    The Dule Revolution

    After months of secret meetings, in the early hours of 22 February the communities attack several police posts at once and drive the officers out. The next day the press reports that “the San Blas Indians are on the warpath”.

  10. March 1925

    The USS Cleveland

    The United States sends the cruiser USS Cleveland. In Ustupu, Ailigandí and Cartí its officers hear both the Guna leaders and the Panamanian authorities, opening the talks that end the conflict.

  11. 1925

    The Declaration of Independence

    The leaders proclaim the Declaration of Independence and Human Rights of the Tule people of San Blas and the Darién, asserting their right to self-determination and denouncing the abuses they had suffered.

  12. 1927

    What they defended

    Two years later, Swedish ethnographer Erland Nordenskiöld photographs island life: molas, cotton spinning, gold nose rings. Exactly what the authorities had tried to ban.

  13. 1930–1953

    From reserve to comarca

    Law 59 of 1930 declares the territory an indigenous reserve. Law 2 of 1938 creates the Comarca of San Blas, and Law 16 of 1953 recognises its own system of government, with the Guna General Congress as the highest authority. It remains one of the most stable indigenous autonomies in Latin America.

  14. Every February

    Living memory

    Every February the communities commemorate the revolution with re-enactments, the warriors' dance and the chicha celebration.

  15. Today

    Guna Yala

    In 1998 the Comarca of San Blas was renamed Kuna Yala and, from 2011, Guna Yala: “Guna land”. Today it faces new challenges: tourism, young people leaving, and rising seas, which in 2024 led the people of Gardi Sugdub to move to the mainland.
